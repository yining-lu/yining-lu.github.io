As internet-based medical and health-information services expand, health risks
affecting adolescents increasingly extend beyond their own media use to the digital
practices of family members. **Parental cyberchondria** — repetitive online searching
for a child's health information accompanied by escalating anxiety — may therefore
constitute an emerging family risk.

Drawing on family systems theory and the social amplification of risk framework, this
study surveyed 273 parent–child dyads (546 participants in total; children aged 8–14,
*M* = 10.27, *SD* = 1.56). Parental cyberchondria significantly predicted more negative
parental evaluations of the child's physical health. These evaluations were associated
with the child's own physical-health assessment, which in turn was associated with the
child's mental health. The total effect of parental cyberchondria on children's mental
health was significant, whereas the direct effect was not; the association operated
mainly through the serial pathway.

The findings show that digital health risks can travel beyond the person exposed to
online information and form a cross-person "digital ripple" through family
interaction. Adolescent health promotion should therefore incorporate families into
upstream prevention — improving parents' digital health literacy, reducing
catastrophic interpretations and overprotection, strengthening constructive health
communication, and increasing protective factors such as regular physical activity,
stable routines, joint information appraisal, and positive health narratives.
