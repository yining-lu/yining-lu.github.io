**Introduction.** Smartphones have become increasingly embedded in everyday family
interactions. Although prior research has documented negative effects of parental
smartphone use during family interaction (i.e., *parental phubbing*) on adolescents'
mental health, it remains unclear whether these associations reflect stable differences
*between* families or fluctuations that unfold over time *within* everyday family
dynamics, especially from a family communication system perspective.

**Methods.** The present study utilized three-wave longitudinal survey data from 1,753
Chinese adolescents (*M*age = 14.93, *SD* = 2.78; 51.61% female). A Random-Intercept
Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) was employed to explore the relationships between
parental phubbing, parent–adolescent closeness, and adolescents' well-being at both
between- and within-person levels.

**Results.** At the between-person level, parental phubbing was negatively associated
with parent–adolescent closeness and adolescents' well-being. However, at the
within-person level, it was adolescents' well-being that predicted subsequent
relational dynamics over time. For fathers, higher adolescent well-being predicted
greater father–adolescent closeness, which in turn predicted lower father phubbing. For
mothers, higher adolescent well-being directly predicted lower mother phubbing and
greater mother–adolescent closeness over time. Neither father nor mother phubbing
significantly predicted subsequent changes in adolescents' well-being at the
within-person level.

**Conclusion.** Parental phubbing not only functions as a risk factor for adolescents'
mental health, but may also reflect *parental reactive behavior* within everyday family
interactions — highlighting implications for future family-based interventions.
