When adolescents witness cyberbullying, most do nothing. This line of work asks what
separates the bystanders who act from those who scroll past, and how the design of
reporting mechanisms shapes that decision.

In a published review, my colleagues and I synthesized the factors influencing
**bystander behavior in adolescent cyberbullying**. Building on that, an ongoing study
distinguishes **bully-directed from victim-directed** bystander responses — defending
the target and confronting the perpetrator are psychologically distinct acts — and
examines how each relates to formal reporting intentions.

A second strand grew out of fieldwork. As a core research assistant at Zhejiang
University, I spent a month in Liuyang, Hunan and Mianyang, Sichuan interviewing more
than forty **left-behind children**, and helped develop scales of *perceived
separation* and *media compensation* for this population. That experience shaped my
conviction that quantitative instruments should be grounded in sustained qualitative
contact with the people they are meant to measure.
