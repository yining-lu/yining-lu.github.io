Much of the digital well-being literature treats connectivity as the variable of
interest. I am more interested in its inverse: the deliberate, effortful practice of
**disconnection**, and what it reveals about people's underlying psychological needs.

One longitudinal project follows adolescents' mobile disconnection over time, testing
whether disconnection supports **basic psychological needs** and, through them,
resilience. A second project uses **experience sampling** to capture smartphone
practice as it unfolds in everyday face-to-face interaction — the moment-to-moment
experience of phubbing, distraction, and guilt — with attention to how these
experiences are unequally distributed across social groups. Framing this as a question
of *digital inequality* rather than individual self-control shifts the burden away from
willpower and toward the conditions that make disconnection possible in the first
place.
