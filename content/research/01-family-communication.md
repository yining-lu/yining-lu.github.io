Smartphones have become an unremarkable presence in family life, yet their
consequences for parent–child relationships are far from neutral. This line of work
asks how routine, low-visibility media behaviors — a parent glancing at a phone
mid-conversation, an adolescent withdrawing into a device — accumulate into
meaningful shifts in family closeness and adolescent mental health.

In one project, my collaborators and I use longitudinal data to examine
**parental phubbing**: how being snubbed by a parent's phone use predicts
parent–adolescent closeness over time, and how that closeness in turn shapes
adolescents' well-being. A related study looks at the interplay of
**digital disconnection, digital literacy, and parental active mediation**, asking
which parental strategies actually buffer adolescents against the mental health costs
of heavy connectivity.

Across these projects I am especially interested in *directionality* — whether media
behaviors erode relationships, or strained relationships drive media behaviors — which
is why I favor longitudinal and experience-sampling designs over cross-sectional
snapshots.
