Most accounts of phubbing treat it as a matter of individual self-control. This project
instead asks how everyday smartphone practice is **unequally distributed** — and what
that means for the quality of face-to-face interaction.

Using **experience sampling**, the study captures moment-to-moment reports of phubbing,
distraction, and guilt as they occur in daily life, modeling them at two levels:
between-person differences associated with socioeconomic status, and within-person
fluctuations that unfold across situations. The design also accounts for the lagged
autoregressive effect of guilt, allowing the analysis to distinguish momentary
experience from stable disposition.

Framing this as a question of *digital inequality* rather than willpower shifts the
burden away from individual restraint and toward the social conditions that make
sustained attention possible in the first place.
